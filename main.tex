\begin{abstract}
Side-channel defenses for LLM serving and agents are evaluated in one setting and then reused in another: a different receiver, a different execution path, a differently trained attacker. We argue that three boundaries decide whether such reuse is sound: the \emph{receiving view} (what the observer sees after the last emitter), the \emph{affected state} (whether the defense only discards outputs or executes actions that change state), and the \emph{attacker and workload} (whether the attacker was fitted on defended outputs, and on which population). Observer specification and adaptive attacks are established principles in website fingerprinting and adversarial-ML evaluation; the affected-state boundary appears once a defense issues cover actions through an agent's tools, and those traditions have no counterpart for it.

We turn the boundaries into a six-field reporting card whose output, for each claim, is a supported comparison, an incompatible one, or a named missing measurement, and we fill it completely for a published cache defense. Two case studies show that the attacker boundary and the affected-state boundary can each reverse a conclusion. In a synthetic TLS replay, response batching drives a clean-trained attacker to chance balanced accuracy (0.500, AUROC 0.678), yet an attacker trained on batched outputs reaches 0.732 (AUROC 0.773), close to the undefended 0.744 (0.800). In a retail-agent sandbox, read cover produces no detectable change in task success ($+0.7$ points, 95\% interval $[-4.9, 6.1]$) while side-effecting write cover lowers it by 46.9 points, although both discard the cover response. The card is a proposal: we have not measured whether it improves analysts' decisions, and we specify the study that would.
\end{abstract}
