\section{Threat Model}\label{sec:threat}
\paragraph{Assets and adversary}
The private target is the user's requested task, here one of six support actions. The defender controls the Agent and its controller, and may run relays, but does not control third-party tool services. We consider three observers, distinguished by their rights. A \emph{transit observer} sees connection destinations, directions, ciphertext lengths and times on the links it watches. A \emph{recipient} additionally reads the requests addressed to it, including arguments. An \emph{active auditor} also chooses probes. Identifying a requested action does not establish whether it was authorized; that needs the principal and policy context. \paragraph{When cover must reach the application}
Transport cover ends at an endpoint the defender controls, after the observer and before the application~\cite{apthorpe2019stp}. A relay outside a monitored egress can serve as that endpoint, but the hop from the relay to a third-party service remains. On that hop, the service's own protocol stack still offers two terminators the defender can use without the provider's cooperation. TLS~1.3 lets the sender pad any record and send padding-only records, which the receiver's TLS layer discards~\cite[Sec.~5.4]{rfc8446}; HTTP/2 lets the sender pad DATA and HEADERS frames and send PING frames, which the receiver's HTTP layer consumes~\cite[Secs.~6.1, 6.7]{rfc9113}. These mechanisms can shape the defender's own request bytes and timing. They cannot make the service send a response: a padding record elicits nothing, and a PING elicits only an immediate eight-octet acknowledgement. Response presence, size and service latency are produced by the application.

Whether cover must reach the application therefore depends on the privacy target. If the observer may learn which request--response exchanges occur, their response sizes or their service latencies, protocol-level padding leaves those signals and cover must elicit real application responses (Table~\ref{tab:cover-necessity}). If only request-side shape matters, padding suffices. A recipient that operates the service sees every exchange it receives, so against it cover must also carry plausible arguments. Reaching the application does not by itself require a business write: a read-only or no-op call elicits a real response. Cover must perform a state-changing operation only if such operations produce responses or latencies that reads cannot imitate. We call any cover that reaches the application \emph{executed cover}; the design space in Section~\ref{sec:design} separates how much business work it performs.

\paragraph{Observer in this paper}
Our measurements use a transit observer on the final hop to seven tool services, with no defender relay in between, and a privacy target that includes the exchange sequence and response timing. It receives destinations, ciphertext bytes and TLS record completion times. Section~\ref{sec:dispatch} additionally supplies episode and call windows, an evaluator privilege that Section~\ref{sec:noboundary} withdraws. The recipient view, where decoy arguments are also visible, is part of the threat model but not of these measurements (Section~\ref{sec:limits}). The adversary may train on traffic generated under the same cover policy; the attack suite therefore includes classifiers and cover-removal models fitted on protected training executions, alongside attacks frozen on unprotected traffic.
