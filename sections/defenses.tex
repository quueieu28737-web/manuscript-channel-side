\subsection{What a defense changes}
Table~\ref{tab:defense-space} organizes six intervention choices by enforcement point, trust, cost and residual signal. Association defenses hide a destination or its link to a user. Padding changes sizes, while scheduling changes temporal structure. Transport cover can terminate before business execution; execution cover adds real work. State isolation limits shared-resource signals, and content minimization limits what a recipient learns. These choices can be combined, but their costs and remaining views must be evaluated together.

\subsection{Relating communication defenses to Agent execution}
STP illustrates transport cover that can terminate at a tunnel endpoint~\cite{apthorpe2019stp}; Walkie-Talkie and FRONT provide shaping mechanisms~\cite{wang2017walkietalkie,gong2020front}. For the ticket Agent, this could mean padding the tool response at an egress proxy, pacing model tokens, or sending cover to a trusted terminator. Calling the refund tool as a decoy would instead cross the business-state boundary. The intervention must therefore specify both where cover is removed and what work it can trigger. Constant-rate shaping in the style of BuFLO and Tamaraw~\cite{dyer2012peekaboo,cai2014tamaraw} can incur high idle-time costs in model-driven workloads. Model waits create gaps that a fixed-rate defense must fill, and streamed responses can exceed its minimum schedule. In our pilot, joint model-link shaping cost 19.5--22.5$\times$ bytes, and 1,818 of 2,736 shaped responses overran the minimum schedule, without a consistent improvement across the reported recognition and localization point estimates (Section~\ref{sec:live-study}).

The measured design points occupy different parts of this space. WRA changes executed research, live OHTTP changes destination association, and the paired forwarding study changes the context sent to the model. Section~\ref{sec:evidence} gives the source-specific recovery and cost results; Section~\ref{sec:experiment} tests cover execution, forwarding and connection policies. Supplement S7 retains the complete defense map, including identity obfuscation and adversarial-Agent blocking as distinct objectives.

\subsection{Choosing a layer in practice}
Start from the private object and the observer's rights. Protect destination associations when contacted domains expose the query; protect visible model output sizes and timing when those carry the target; minimize sensitive arguments when the recipient is the observer. Then test what remains on other permitted links. A shared gateway changes which tasks contribute to a connection, so its connection policy belongs in the evaluation. For executed cover, bind privacy, token/tool use, requested-task completion and whole-state correctness to the same protected runs. Compare proxy and isolated execution under the same allowed observations. The cover case shows why a size-only result needs a removal attack: client-receipt timing separates fast controller insertions from model-driven calls (Section~\ref{sec:cover-study}). These choices turn the risk map into a deployment-specific protection plan.
